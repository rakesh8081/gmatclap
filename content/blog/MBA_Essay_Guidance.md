**The essays that impress admissions committees aren't always the most polished. They're the ones that reveal the person behind the profile.**

Every MBA applicant has achievements worth talking about.

Promotions. Leadership experiences. Awards. Successful projects. Difficult challenges. Career transitions.

Yet, when applicants sit down to write their essays, many make the same mistake: **they try too hard to sound impressive.**

They use sophisticated vocabulary. They describe every achievement. They turn ordinary experiences into dramatic stories. They try to sound like the ideal MBA candidate.

And somewhere in the process, their personality disappears.

### Your essay has a different job than your résumé

Your résumé tells the Admissions Committee what you have done.

Your test scores demonstrate your academic ability. Your transcripts show your academic history. Your recommendations provide another perspective on your professional capabilities.

**Your essay must reveal what these documents cannot.**

Who are you beyond your achievements?

What motivates you?

What do you value?

How do you respond when things don't go as planned?

What have you learned about yourself?

What kind of person will your classmates meet on campus?

A strong essay doesn't repeat your profile. It adds a dimension to it.

If your résumé says you led a team, your essay could reveal what you learned when someone challenged your decision.

If your résumé says you launched a product, your essay could explore the uncertainty, the mistake, or the difficult decision that changed how you approach leadership.

The achievement is the headline.

**The personal insight is the story.**

### Stop trying to sound impressive. Start being interesting.

Consider these two statements:

**Weak:**

*"The experience enhanced my leadership capabilities and taught me the importance of resilience, adaptability, and effective communication."*

**Stronger:**

*"I had prepared answers for every question, except the one my teammate asked: 'Why didn't you listen to us before making the decision?'"*

The second sentence creates a moment.

It raises a question. It reveals vulnerability. It gives the reader something to remember.

You don't need sophisticated vocabulary to impress an admissions committee.

You need clarity, honesty, specificity, and reflection.

A simple sentence describing an uncomfortable truth about yourself can be more powerful than an entire paragraph of leadership jargon.

### Small moments often reveal more than big achievements

Applicants naturally gravitate towards their biggest accomplishments.

Winning a competition. Building a business. Leading a transformation. Managing a crisis.

But the most revealing moments are often much smaller.

The moment you noticed that someone in your team had stopped speaking.

The moment you realised your parents had sacrificed something you had never fully understood.

The moment you failed to defend a teammate because you were afraid of upsetting your manager.

The moment you received criticism and initially became defensive.

The moment you changed your opinion after listening to someone whose experience was completely different from yours.

These moments may not look impressive on a résumé.

But they can reveal empathy, maturity, self-awareness, values and character.

Consider this:

**Generic:**

*"I learned that leadership is about listening."*

**Specific:**

*"I realised leadership wasn't about being the first person to speak. It was about noticing who hadn't spoken at all."*

The difference is not better vocabulary.

The difference is a genuine observation.

**Specific beats generic. Every single time.**

### Details make essays memorable

A good essay allows the reader to imagine being inside your experience.

Compare:

*"I was nervous before my presentation."*

With:

*"I reread the opening paragraph five times while waiting outside the auditorium. My hands were shaking, and I had begun wondering whether I had prepared enough."*

The second version feels human.

You don't need to fill your essay with descriptive details. A few meaningful details can make an entire story come alive.

A conversation you still remember.

A sentence someone said to you.

A decision you struggled to make.

A mistake you made.

A thought you were embarrassed to admit.

A small change in your behaviour afterwards.

**Details create credibility. Reflection creates meaning.**

### The story isn't what happened. It's why it mattered.

Many applicants narrate their experiences chronologically:

This happened.

Then I did this.

Then we achieved this.

Then I learned this lesson.

But narration alone doesn't create reflection.

The Admissions Committee wants to understand:



- Why did this experience affect you?
- What assumption did it challenge?
- What did you initially get wrong?
- Why did you react the way you did?
- What did you discover about yourself?
- How has this changed your behaviour?



For example:

*"I failed to convince the team to adopt my strategy."*

That is an event.

But consider the reflection:

*"For years, I had equated being prepared with being persuasive. I assumed that if my analysis was strong enough, people would naturally agree. The failure forced me to confront the fact that I had spent more time defending my recommendation than understanding the concerns behind the disagreement."*

Now we understand something deeper.

The applicant has not merely experienced failure.

**They have examined themselves.**

Reflection turns an event into an insight.

Insight reveals character.

### A simple framework for writing a personal essay

Use this framework to develop your narrative:

**MOMENT → EXPERIENCE → REFLECTION → GROWTH → FUTURE**

**1. Moment:** Begin with a specific moment, decision, conflict, conversation or observation.

**2. Experience:** Explain what happened, your role, the challenge and the choices you made.

**3. Reflection:** Explore why the experience mattered. What did it reveal or challenge?

**4. Growth:** Explain what changed in your thinking, behaviour, priorities or values.

**5. Future:** Connect that insight to the person you want to become and, where relevant, your MBA journey.

This isn't a rigid formula. But it helps prevent a common problem: writing an essay that describes a lot of activity without revealing much about the applicant.

The experience is the vehicle.

**The reflection is the destination.**

### Your MBA essay is not a résumé in paragraph form

One of the biggest mistakes applicants make is trying to include everything.

Every leadership position.

Every award.

Every internship.

Every important project.

Every career milestone.

The result is often an essay that feels like a compressed résumé.

The problem isn't that these achievements are unimportant.

The problem is that they compete for space with the insights that make your story personal.

Choose one or two experiences that genuinely support the central message of your essay.

Selectivity is a form of storytelling.

The stories you choose—and the stories you leave out—help define the person the Admissions Committee sees.

### "Why this school?" requires research, not flattery

*"Your university has world-class faculty, a diverse student community, a strong alumni network and an excellent reputation."*

This could describe dozens of business schools.

A convincing "Why MBA?" or "Why this school?" essay connects:

**Your past → Your present gap → Your future goal → The school's specific resources**

Research courses, professors, experiential learning opportunities, clubs, industry ecosystems and initiatives relevant to your goals.

But don't merely list them.

Explain why they matter to your development.

Show that you understand what you need from the MBA—and why this particular programme is positioned to help you achieve it.

The difference between a generic essay and a convincing one is often the difference between browsing a school's website and genuinely understanding how the school fits into your journey.

### Write the first draft badly

Many applicants spend hours trying to write the perfect opening sentence.

They delete it. Rewrite it. Change the vocabulary. Rewrite it again.

And sometimes, after two hours, they have one polished sentence and no essay.

Don't begin by trying to impress anyone.

Begin by getting the story onto the page.

Write the full draft without worrying about grammar, word count, transitions or whether the opening is perfect.

Then return to it and ask:



- What is the most meaningful moment in this story?
- Which sentence reveals something genuine about me?
- Where am I explaining an achievement instead of reflecting on it?
- Which paragraphs can be removed?
- Does this essay sound like something I would actually say?



The first draft is for discovery.

The editing process is for clarity.

**A messy first draft often contains something a perfectly planned essay never will: your actual thoughts.**

### Don't edit away your personality

MBA essays should absolutely be edited.

Grammar matters. Structure matters. Clarity matters. Word limits matter.

But excessive editing can make an essay sound like everyone else's.

The applicant's original voice disappears beneath polished phrases, consultant vocabulary and generic conclusions.

Seek feedback. Challenge your ideas. Improve your structure.

But the final essay must remain yours.

A useful test is to ask someone who knows you well:

*"Does this essay sound like me?"*

Not:

*"Does this essay sound impressive?"*

The first question helps preserve your personality.

The second can encourage you to become someone you're not.

### The ultimate test of your essay

Before submitting, ask yourself:

**Could another applicant with a similar résumé write this essay?**

If yes, you probably need more personal details, deeper reflection or a more distinctive perspective.

**Am I telling the reader something they cannot already learn from my résumé?**

If not, reconsider what your essay is adding.

**Have I shown my qualities instead of simply naming them?**

Replace declarations with experiences, decisions, actions and observations.

**Does the essay sound like me?**

Read it aloud.

If you would never use a particular sentence in a conversation, question whether it belongs in your essay.

### The essay that gets remembered

Imagine reading hundreds of MBA applications.

You have seen impressive scores, ambitious career goals, leadership positions, awards and successful projects.

Then you read an essay about a student who made a mistake, struggled to understand why, reconsidered an assumption and changed the way they treated people.

Or an essay about a conversation with a parent that changed the applicant's understanding of success.

Or a story about a seemingly insignificant moment that revealed something profound about their values.

You may not remember every achievement in every application.

**But you might remember that person.**

That is the power of a personal essay.

Your MBA essay doesn't need to be flawless. It doesn't need to sound like a bestselling novel. It doesn't need to demonstrate that you have already become the perfect leader.

It needs to be honest, thoughtful, specific and unmistakably yours.

Your achievements tell the Admissions Committee what you have accomplished.

Your essay tells them **who accomplished those things—and who that person is becoming.**

And when your essay succeeds in doing that, it becomes much more than an application requirement.

**It becomes an introduction.**
